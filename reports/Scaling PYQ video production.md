# Turn plans into data, then scale

Ten two-minute videos per operator per day is reachable, but not by buying a faster renderer: it is reachable only when the plan for a video stops being code (`plan_<video>.py` plus `build_<video>.sh`) and becomes a small, schema-validated data file over a closed catalogue of beat templates, reviewed as a contact sheet before anything renders. Every published system that works splits planner, renderer and critic around an explicit storyboard, and the one that runs at production scale (Baidu's LASEV) compiles a structured script through fixed templates rather than generating code ([arXiv 2602.11790](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11790v1)). HyperFrames is a credible render engine for that design and worth a one-day spike, but it solves rendering, which you have already automated, not planning; adopt it pinned and under your own templates, never as free-form agent-written HTML. The three manual steps around the presenter (download, key calibration, landscape reframing) and the Flow browser extension are removable on paper through HeyGen's v3 API and the paid Gemini API, at roughly **$3 to $11 of vendor charges per video**, dominated by the avatar engine; two facts in that chain are unverified and each costs one API call to test. On hardware, the 8 GB of RAM is the fault, not the M2's speed: buy **one Mac mini M6 24 GB / 512 GB at about Rs 1,47,900** now, fix the composite in software, measure, and only then decide on a second node. The honest throughput verdict is that today's machine cannot do ten a day even with zero re-renders, that no independently verified benchmark exists for this kind of composited video at that rate, and that the target holds for template-covered videos once five conditions set out below are true. Many numbers in the underlying notes are secondary-sourced or read through a summarising fetch; this report marks them where they matter and does not treat them as settled.

## Ten a day is a 36-minute attention budget, and today's box spends 6 to 10 hours on compositing alone

Start with the operator. Ten videos in a six-hour productive day is **36 minutes of human attention per video**; in a full eight hours it is 48 minutes with no slack. Everything a person does to a video (script review in the Google Doc, presenter acquisition, plan approval, final watch, any fix) has to fit inside that. Today each video gets a hand-written plan and build script authored by an engineer in Claude Code, so the plan alone exceeds the whole budget. Planning has to fall by roughly an order of magnitude, and it can only do that if the operator's job changes from authoring to selecting.

Now the machine. A 90-second part takes 26 to 47 minutes to composite, which is 17 to 31 times realtime. Ten two-minute videos is 20 finished minutes, so compositing alone needs **5.7 to 10.3 hours per operator per day on the current M2**, serial, one heavy job at a time, before Manim or HTML rendering and before a single re-render. One re-render per video doubles it past a working day. The hardware note's planning figure for five operators, which counts multi-part videos and a 1.5x re-render factor (both assumptions), is about 340 rendered minutes a day: roughly 140 machine-hours at today's speed, 17 hours at 3x realtime, 8.5 hours at 1.5x. The 3x and 1.5x figures are targets, not measurements. The implication is still clear: the software fix is worth a factor of about eight, and no hardware on the market is.

Published throughput evidence does not rescue the target; it only says the target is not absurd. The best-documented case is a single instructional designer producing 200+ avatar-only micro-videos in about six months, under two a day, on a far simpler product, and that is a vendor case study ([Synthesia: Zoom](https://www.synthesia.io/case-studies/zoom)). Vendor blogs claim 10 to 30 short clips a day with avatar tools, read only from search snippets ([Frameo](https://frameo.ai/blog/ai-video-tool-content-production-capacity/)). The only hard quality data is Baidu's preprint: fully automatic, 96% publishable, but **only 58% "perfect"**, with 38% carrying minor presentation issues and 4% critical errors ([arXiv 2602.11790](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11790v1)). Read that as the realistic ceiling for automation: about four plans in ten will still want a human touch, so the touch must take seconds.

Ten a day therefore requires five things to be true at once. The plan is data over fixed templates, so review is accept, pick an alternative, toggle a beat to "none", or drag an anchor. Every decision is made before the render starts, on stills and an animatic, so renders are unattended and not repeated. A correction re-renders one beat, not one part. The presenter arrives by API rather than by a person recording, downloading and calibrating a key. And anything the template set cannot express is escalated to the engineer instead of being solved in the operator's queue. Under those conditions an indicative budget is 5 to 8 minutes of script review, 3 to 5 minutes of plan approval, 3 to 4 minutes of final watch and a few minutes of fixes on the four-in-ten that need them, comfortably inside 36. That budget is a design target from the notes' reasoning, not a measurement; the first thing the new system should record is median operator minutes per video and first-pass yield.

## The plan becomes data: closed templates, named slots, and critics that run before any pixel

### The literature has converged on your shape and says where the leverage is

Your visual director, beats file and render stage already match the architecture every working system uses. What the papers add is the ranking of what matters. In Code2Video's ablations, removing the planner cost about 41 points on its teaching benchmark, removing the curated asset database about 30, removing the discrete placement anchors 26.8, and removing the critic 21.3 ([arXiv 2510.01174](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01174)). The plan is worth about twice the critic. In LASEV, a rubric-based semantic critic added 40 points of publishable rate, a compile/execute critic 22, and a rule critic 10 ([arXiv 2602.11790](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11790v1)). Free-form Manim generation, by contrast, succeeds about 7% of the time on the first attempt and reaches 93% only after five repair rounds, with element layout its lowest-scoring dimension ([arXiv 2502.19400v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.19400v2)). These figures were extracted through a summarising fetch rather than read from the tables and should be spot-checked before being quoted outside the team; the direction is consistent across four independent papers.

Two findings map directly onto habits you already have. Code2Video places elements on a **6x6 grid of anchors** and found a finer 8x8 grid performed worse ([arXiv 2510.01174](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01174)), which is the general form of your anchor-table-by-name rule: the planner chooses among named slots and never emits a coordinate. And Paper2Video found that models are insensitive to fine numeric corrections, so it renders several layout variants and has a vision model pick one ([arXiv 2510.05096](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05096v2)). That converts review from describing a fix to choosing from three stills, which is exactly what a low-technical operator can do.

None of the surveyed systems is conditioned on a pre-recorded narration with word timings, handles Devanagari, or traces a textbook figure. Those are your real constraints and the literature does not address them. No direct benchmark compares a constrained scene spec against free-form generation on the same tasks either; the case for templates rests on cross-paper comparison.

### What the plan file should look like

One `plan.json` per part. A `strategy` field from the closed set `question_strategy()` already produces. A list of beats, each carrying a cue expressed as caption line and word index, a `template` from a closed enum, a named `slot`, a small `props` object, a `presenter` mode and a `motion`. **`template: "none"` is a first-class value**, so "no graphic" is a choice the schema forces at every window rather than something the model must remember. Timing never appears in the plan; it is resolved from the alignment file, which keeps "narration sets the clock" true by construction. The template catalogue is the set you already render: counted list that fills in, numbered table, definition, comparison columns, derivation step with symbol legend, traced figure with anchor-named labels, photo, Veo panel, question card, answer page. Your chip renderer's points/table/define/cols/math/svg/photo modes are most of this catalogue under different names.

Claude's structured outputs guarantee schema-valid JSON on the current model line, but the grammar has hard limits that a naive schema will hit: 24 optional parameters and 16 union-typed parameters per request, no numeric ranges, no recursion, and enum capitalisation is not guaranteed ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). The design consequences are concrete. Make every field required and use `"none"` instead of optionality. Keep the beat flat with a template enum and a generic props object, then validate template-specific props in code. Use lower_snake_case ASCII names. Plan one part per request. Keep the schema byte-stable so the 24-hour grammar cache and the prompt cache both hit.

A closed enum also makes the rule critic nearly free. "The question says सचित्र and the plan has no traced-figure beat" is a JSON query. So are quiet ratio, caption repetition, longest blank window, longest frozen window, and string provenance (every on-screen string must derive from the script, answer or label set, so the planner cannot introduce a fact). Free-form codegen survives only behind a beat tagged `custom`, mirroring LASEV's optional illustration component, and a `custom` beat routes to the engineer. The engineer's job changes from per-video planner to template author.

### Critics: geometry in code, semantics in the model, humans as the write-gate

Do not gate layout on a vision model. On slide images the best models reach about 0.72 F1 at finding elements and their geometry error stays around 0.55 (1 minus IoU), and coarse five-point scales are more consistent than 100-point ones ([arXiv 2510.22045](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.22045)). ManimAgent found its vision score correlated with blind human scores at r = -0.17 and said that this prevents using such scores as primary evidence ([arXiv 2606.30296](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.30296v1)). Your renderers already know every element's box, so overlap, clipping, caption-band and presenter-floor violations are computable from the resolved plan with complete recall before rendering, and with named slots most collisions cannot be expressed at all. Reserve the model for what geometry cannot answer, asked as one binary question per defect on the contact sheet: does this leader end on the part it names, does the figure match the question. No benchmark exists for vision-model detection of Devanagari mis-shaping, the silent failure you have hit, so measure that on your own known-bad frames before trusting it.

Retrieval helps only when it retrieves your own work. Generic documentation retrieval cut success from 93.8% to 82.1% in one study ([arXiv 2502.19400v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.19400v2)), while a memory of the system's own approved outputs and validated failures lifted first-pass rate from 62.0% to 84.9% and roughly halved repair rounds within 200 entries ([arXiv 2606.30296](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.30296v1)). That second result comes from 33 probe tasks with chance-level rater agreement, so treat the size as indicative. You already hold better raw material than that paper had: about 70 human-approved videos as the positive channel, and the bug ledger and memory notes as the negative one. Key retrieval exactly on subject, chapter, strategy and marks first, keep two or three examples, and promote every pitfall that can be expressed as a check into code.

### Timing and figures are the two planning inputs worth hardening

Cue resolution depends on word timings, and the evidence against ASR-derived timestamps is strong for English: on TIMIT, MFA put 91.6% of word boundaries within 50 ms, an MMS CTC aligner 63.7%, and WhisperX 15.6% ([arXiv 2606.18466](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.18466)). A second paper reports WhisperX at 82.4% within 50 ms on the same corpus ([arXiv 2606.10675](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.10675)); the first attributes the gap to which boundary is measured. The two agree that a dedicated aligner beats WhisperX and disagree on by how much. **No published Hindi word-boundary accuracy exists for any aligner**, so this must be settled on your own audio. Because the script is known, this is forced alignment, and cheap candidates exist: a local CTC aligner on a Hindi acoustic model ([parseh/aligner-hi](https://huggingface.co/parseh/aligner-hi)), and ElevenLabs' hosted forced alignment, which lists Hindi ([ElevenLabs docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/forced-alignment)). A gold set of about 300 hand-marked cue words on real HeyGen audio is an afternoon and replaces every assumption.

Figures are the other hand-placed input. Tracing remains correct: on a 2026 benchmark of figure vectorisation, a classical tracer had the best pixel fidelity by a wide margin (LPIPS 0.092 against 0.21 to 0.38 for generative models), while the generative models' advantage was editable structure ([arXiv 2603.24575](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24575v1)). That benchmark is clean digital figures with Latin text; nothing evaluated scanned Hindi line art, and no benchmark exists for associating labels with parts. The practical route is a per-chapter figure library: each traced figure stored once with its anchor table, auto-detected anchors corrected in a drag-a-dot screen, then reused for every question on that chapter with only the label cues re-resolved against the new audio. Code2Video's result that a curated asset database was the second-largest contributor supports investing here ([arXiv 2510.01174](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01174)).

## One Chromium renderer for layout; HyperFrames is an engine to trial, not a planning fix

### What the frameworks actually offer

Three render paths is one too many for the content you make. Captions, question card, lists, tables, labelled SVG figures, KaTeX lines, the Veo panel and the answer page are layout and typography. A Chromium renderer shapes Devanagari and lays out KaTeX in the same DOM line, which removes the "Hindi and maths cannot mix" rule by construction, with one documented caveat: Brahmic glyphs inside a formula's `\text{}` fall back to system fonts with approximate metrics, so Hindi belongs in ordinary HTML around the formula ([KaTeX docs](https://katex.org/docs/supported.html)). Your `tools/edu/renderer` already works this way.

HyperFrames is the tool you asked about, and it is real: Apache-2.0, 58,410 stars, v0.8.140 released 7 October 2026, with a seek-based renderer that never calls play, embeds fonts locally and forbids wall-clock time and network at render ([heygen-com/hyperframes](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes); [HeyGen research](https://www.heygen.com/research/html-to-video)). It ships `preview`, `lint`, `snapshot` and `render`, a Studio with timeline and caption editors, and render modes for local, Docker, Lambda and Cloud Run ([HyperFrames rendering guide](https://hyperframes.heygen.com/guides/rendering)). Four limitations matter for you specifically. Fully deterministic capture works only on Linux; macOS falls back to screenshot capture with heuristic timing. Embedded video is pre-extracted to JPEG frames and video-heavy compositions fall back to a single worker ([HeyGen research](https://www.heygen.com/research/html-to-video)). No green-screen chroma key was found in its docs; its `remove-background` command is a matting model, which is not a substitute for keying a real green plate ([HyperFrames guide](https://hyperframes.heygen.com/guides/remove-background.md)). And it is 0.x with near-daily releases plus an open bug in which the default worker count takes a slow path ([issue #4584](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/issues/4584)).

Remotion is the mature alternative: 62,337 stars, a first-party `colorKey()` chroma-key effect with spill suppression, native alpha video, and Lambda rendering with published costs of about $0.017 for a one-minute video in about 19 seconds ([Remotion colorKey](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/effects/color-key); [Lambda cost examples](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/lambda/cost-example)). It costs a company licence above three people, $0.01 per render with a $100 monthly minimum for automated pipelines ([remotion.pro](https://www.remotion.pro/license)), and it requires recasting your HTML pages as React components.

**No measured Apple Silicon render time at 1080x1920 exists in public sources for either framework.** The only numbers are a trivial HyperFrames clip on a Linux RTX 3090 box at about 5.8x realtime and one third-party Remotion measurement on an unspecified cloud VM at 0.43x realtime, on different content and hardware, neither with a presenter video ([issue #4584](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/issues/4584); [Your_Editor PR #12](https://github.com/mahmoudemad68/Your_Editor/pull/12)). They bracket expectations; they do not rank the tools.

### The recommendation

The research notes split on this: the framework survey ranks Remotion first on determinism and in-framework keying, and HyperFrames first on reuse and licence. My position follows from one design decision that makes most of the disagreement moot: **keep the presenter out of the browser.** Render the graphics layer in Chromium, and overlay a pre-keyed or alpha presenter in one short ffmpeg pass. That sidesteps HyperFrames' weakest area (embedded video and single-worker fallback) and makes Remotion's main advantage (in-composition keying) unnecessary.

With that settled, adopt as follows. **Keep** the home-grown HTML renderer's page code as the template runtime, because it already renders chips from JSON specs and that is the correct pattern. **Trial HyperFrames as the engine underneath it**, replacing your screenshot loop, pinned to an exact version, and adopt it only if a one-day spike passes on Devanagari plus KaTeX correctness, byte-identical frames across two runs, and wall time and RAM on the real machine. Its value to you is the tooling you would otherwise maintain yourself: seekable determinism, lint, single-frame snapshot for contact sheets, live preview, and Lambda. Do not adopt its authoring culture; its skills lean toward agent-written free-form HTML, which is the opposite of a deterministic template factory. Treat its Studio timeline as a correction tool at most, since dragging clips contradicts "narration sets the clock". If the spike fails on macOS determinism, run it in its Docker mode or on Lambda before abandoning it. **Hold Remotion as the fallback** if the spike fails outright.

**Shrink Manim to a specialist.** It stays for true 3D and for derivations that need matched-term morphing, rendered as clips and placed like any other asset, off the per-video critical path. Upgrade to v0.21.0, which reports a roughly 2x faster Cairo renderer and adds Typst mobjects ([Manim releases](https://github.com/ManimCommunity/manim/releases)); whether Typst sets Devanagari and maths together is untested. Do not wait for a GPU renderer: a WebGPU renderer PR was closed unmerged in September 2026 ([PR #4968](https://github.com/ManimCommunity/manim/pull/4968)). Simple "term moves across the equals sign" derivations can move to HTML through KaTeX plus a FLIP-style tween, but no maintained library packages that, so it is build work.

**Drop** Motion Canvas and Revideo as a base (no tagged Motion Canvas release since December 2024; Revideo's steward has moved to another product) ([motion-canvas](https://github.com/motion-canvas/motion-canvas); [YC: Midrender](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/midrender)). Drop template SaaS such as Shotstack and Creatomate: they cannot do the maths and diagram work. Drop the per-video `plan_*.py` and `build_*.sh` scripts as a category once the plan schema lands, and with them most of the 133 files in `tools/`.

### The composite: diagnose it before spending on it

All the keying filters in your recipe (hsvkey, despill, erosion, blend, overlay) are slice-threaded in the installed ffmpeg 8.1, while `format`, `scale` and `crop` are not; this was checked on the machine during research ([ffmpeg filters documentation](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)). A composite that leaves the CPU mostly idle is therefore waiting on something serial: auto-inserted pixel-format conversions between filters, the three-thread cap, or swap. That diagnosis is engineering judgement, not measurement, and no published benchmark exists for keyed 1080x1920 compositing in any tool. Two cheap moves follow regardless. Run the graph once with verbose logging and a null output to separate filter cost from encode cost. And key the presenter **once per take** into an alpha intermediate, so re-renders stop re-paying the key; with 7 GB free that intermediate needs external scratch. If HeyGen's alpha WebM passes its test, the key disappears altogether.

## HeyGen v3 and the Gemini API remove four manual steps, if two one-call tests pass

HeyGen's v3 API renders a digital twin in 9:16 at 1080p, accepts externally produced audio through `audio_url`, notifies by webhook, and with `output_format: "webm"` "automatically removes the background and adds an alpha channel" ([Create Video reference](https://developers.heygen.com/reference/create-video.md); [Transparent Background Videos](https://developers.heygen.com/transparent-background-videos.md)). On paper that removes the manual download, the per-clip hsvkey calibration and the landscape-to-portrait crop measurement in one change. The conditions: the twin must have been trained with matting, or the request is rejected, and an older twin would need re-creating. Alpha edge quality on hair, the codec, and whether WebM carries a surcharge are all unverified. The API still returns only an SRT sidecar with no word-level timestamps ([Get Video reference](https://developers.heygen.com/reference/get-video.md)), so caption timing must keep coming from the audio. The v1 and v2 endpoints retire on 1 November 2026, so build on v3 only ([endpoint version comparison](https://developers.heygen.com/endpoint-version-comparison.md)).

The cleanest fix for the SRT problem is to move narration upstream: synthesise it with a TTS that returns alignment, pass that audio to HeyGen, and build captions from the alignment. ElevenLabs' `with-timestamps` endpoint returns per-character start and end times ([SDK docs mirror](https://tessl.io/registry/tessl/npm-elevenlabs/1.59.0/files/docs/text-to-speech.md)); whether that works on its newest models and how it behaves on Devanagari conjuncts is unconfirmed. Sarvam's Bulbul v3 is far cheaper at Rs 30 per 10,000 characters and won the two Hindi comparisons found, but one is vendor-published across eleven languages and the other is a call-centre benchmark with no stated method, and no documentation was found that Sarvam returns timestamps ([Sarvam TTS](https://www.sarvam.ai/text-to-speech); [Caller Digital benchmark](https://caller.digital/blog/indic-tts-benchmark-bulbul-elevenlabs-sarvam-google-ai4bharat-2026)). Narration costs a rounding error either way, so decide on a listening test of three real scripts. This is a change of voice pipeline, so it is optional and later; forced alignment on the existing audio fixes timing without it.

Veo 3.1 is on the paid Gemini API with image-to-video, first-and-last-frame interpolation, up to three reference images and a seven-second extension chain, at $0.05 to $0.60 per output second depending on tier ([Gemini API Veo docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/veo); [Gemini API pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). That maps onto your existing rules: the subject plate goes in as the image input, and the previous clip's last frame becomes the next clip's first. The eight-second ceiling and 16:9 or 9:16 are properties of Veo, not of Flow, and extension is 720p only. The open question is the mark. A reseller's blog quoting Flow's help text says the visible badge applies automatically in India regardless of the setting ([Atlas Cloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/blog/tips/veo-watermark-remover)); that describes Flow, not the developer API. It is likely but **not confirmed** that API output carries only the invisible SynthID. One paid call from your own billing account answers it, and if it comes back clean, the Chrome extension, the bridge on port 8765 and the delogo step can all retire.

Alternatives do not change this picture on the evidence gathered, but coverage was thin: avatar competitors rest on one secondary source each, and many named tools were not reached. Hosted open talking-head models sit between HeyGen's tiers on price while delivering an opaque 480p or 720p clip that reintroduces matting ([InfiniteTalk](https://github.com/MeiGen-AI/InfiniteTalk)). MatAnyone 2 is the current open state of the art for human matting but needs a first-frame mask, shows CUDA-only examples, and ships under the NTU S-Lab licence, which needs a legal read before commercial use ([MatAnyone2 GitHub](https://github.com/pq-yang/MatAnyone2)). Neither is worth pursuing while HeyGen delivers a green plate or alpha.

One structural point outweighs any vendor choice. A Class 12 syllabus has a bounded set of processes, and roughly two thirds of your scripts are equation and graph work that should never reach Veo. A process clip generated once, reviewed once and stored by concept costs nothing and carries no review risk on reuse. Build the clip library alongside the figure library.

## Target architecture: a queue, a contact sheet and a single source of rules

The pipeline that results has one spine. A sheet row becomes a verified answer and a Hindi script, reviewed in the Doc as now. The presenter is requested through HeyGen v3 and arrives by webhook as a portrait alpha clip; until the alpha test passes, the green MP4 is keyed once per take and cached. A forced aligner produces word timings from that audio. The planner emits `plan.json` per part as structured output, with retrieved approved plans for the same chapter and strategy in a cached prefix. Rule, geometry and semantic critics run on the plan. The HTML path renders one still per beat at its end state into a contact sheet, and a scrubbable animatic plays those stills against the real audio; this is the tier that catches label-sync and frozen-screen problems, and it needs no Manim. The operator approves there. Only then do beats render, each cached on a hash of its inputs, followed by a short overlay pass, `tools/gates.py`, and delivery.

Three supporting choices keep this operable without a platform engineer. For orchestration, DBOS is the smallest change: a Postgres-backed library added to the existing Python CLI with no orchestration server, recovery from the last completed step, and queue concurrency limits that express "one heavy render at a time" directly ([DBOS docs](https://docs.dbos.dev/why-dbos); [DBOS queues](https://docs.dbos.dev/python/tutorials/queue-tutorial)). Prefect 3 is the alternative if you would rather not build the approval screen, since it renders a typed form in its UI when a flow pauses ([Prefect docs](https://docs.prefect.io/v3/advanced/interactive)). Temporal and several others were not researched; that is a gap, not a rejection. For the operator surface, extend the existing FastAPI dashboard into a storyboard review, one row per beat with its still, caption and gate results and a bounded set of actions, and write status back to the Google Sheet operators already use. A full timeline editor is the wrong shape here. For the agent, run Claude headless with an allow-list restricted to pipeline commands and a JSON schema on the plan, so it physically cannot write a one-off build script ([Claude Code headless docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/headless)).

That last choice has a consequence for where knowledge lives. The recommended scripted mode, `--bare`, does not load CLAUDE.md or memory, and is slated to become the default for headless runs ([Claude Code headless docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/headless)). Rules that exist only as prose will not reach an automated run. Triage each entry in the bug ledger and the memory notes in order: can a schema reject it, can a gate reject it, can a hook block it, and only if none apply does it stay prose. You already do this in places; make it the rule, and attach the original failure as a fixture to each converted check. Put fonts, sizes and safe-area floors in one token file, since the presenter floor is currently checked in three places.

For final QC, whole-video review by a vision model is now cheap enough to run on every build: Gemini documents roughly 100 to 300 tokens per second of video, so a two-minute video is 12,000 to 36,000 input tokens ([Gemini video understanding](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/video-understanding)). At one frame per second it misses sub-second defects, so it complements the mechanical gates and never replaces them.

## Nine cheap experiments first, then four phases

Almost every open question above resolves with a test that costs under a day. Run these before committing engineering time or capital.

| # | Experiment | Settles | Cost | Pass looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Run the composite graph with verbose logging and a null output; then key one take once to an alpha intermediate and re-run | Where the 26 to 47 minutes go; whether pre-keying alone reaches near realtime | Half a day | Auto-inserted conversions identified; overlay-only pass several times faster |
| 2 | One HeyGen v3 call: existing twin, 9:16, WebM output, external audio | Matting eligibility, alpha edge quality, true price, SRT accuracy from the API | One call | Clean hair edges against a real background; no rejection |
| 3 | One paid Gemini API Veo 3.1 Fast image-to-video call from your own billing account | Whether API output carries a visible mark in India | One call, about $1 | No visible mark, plate preserved |
| 4 | Render the same 90-second part through the current renderer and through pinned HyperFrames on the M2 | Wall time, peak RAM, two-run frame determinism, Devanagari plus KaTeX correctness | One day | Identical frames twice; minutes, not tens of minutes |
| 5 | Hand-mark about 300 cue words; score Whisper, a Hindi CTC aligner and ElevenLabs forced alignment | Which aligner to trust for Hindi | An afternoon | 95% of cue words within 100 ms |
| 6 | Re-express five delivered videos as `plan.json` over the template catalogue | What share of real beats the closed set covers | Two days | Most beats covered; the remainder names the missing templates |
| 7 | Stopwatch an operator approving ten plans from contact sheet plus animatic | Whether review fits a 3 to 5 minute budget | Half a day | Median under 5 minutes |
| 8 | Ask a vision model binary questions on known-bad frames, including mis-shaped Devanagari | Which defects the semantic critic can be trusted with | Half a day | High recall on label-on-wrong-part; documented blind spots |
| 9 | Blind comparison of Avatar III, IV and V on your twin | The largest recurring cost | Three calls | Cheapest engine the owner cannot tell apart |

Experiment 6 is the most important. It measures the one number this whole report depends on and that no source can supply: how much of your real output a closed template set expresses.

The phases follow from the results, and their durations are my judgement, since the notes could not source migration effort. **Phase 0, the first week:** run the experiments, free the disk, order the Mac mini. **Phase 1, roughly a month:** define the plan schema and template catalogue from experiment 6, build the contact sheet and animatic on the HTML path, add the geometry and rule critics, pre-key or switch to alpha, and add per-beat content-addressed caching with every LLM output stored as a file so cache keys stay deterministic. The engineer still drives, but through `plan.json`; the exit test is that no new `plan_*.py` is written. **Phase 2, the following month or so:** put the queue and storyboard review in the dashboard, wire HeyGen v3 and, if experiment 3 passed, the Veo API, build the figure and clip libraries, lock the headless agent to an allow-list, and put a first operator on template-covered videos with `custom` beats escalating. The exit test is one operator sustaining five a day at a measured first-pass yield. **Phase 3:** add a second render node and more operators, turn approved plans into the retrieval memory, add sampled final watch-through with defect categories feeding new gates, and push toward ten.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. "Which tool renders faster" has a boring answer, because rendering was never the constraint: a diagnosed ffmpeg graph, a presenter keyed once or not at all, and 24 GB of RAM plausibly recover most of the machine time, and every framework on the list renders HTML in minutes. The scarce resource is decisions. Each of the roughly 70 videos delivered so far encoded its decisions in code only an engineer could write, and that is why knowledge accumulated in markdown and memory notes instead of in the system. A closed template set inverts it: the planner's choices become enumerable, the critics become queries, review becomes selection, and each approved plan becomes training material for the next. That flywheel, not HyperFrames or a Mac mini, is what makes the tenth video of the day cost less attention than the first.

Three uncertainties deserve respect. The 58%-perfect ceiling means a pipeline judged on first-pass perfection will always look like it is failing; judge it on median operator minutes and escaped defects. Nobody has published a verified ten-a-day figure for a composited, gated, animated video, so you are setting the benchmark, not meeting one. And at target volume the recurring vendor bill, led by the avatar engine, outruns the hardware within a month, which makes experiment 9 worth more than any purchase in this report.
